Cards, attention, and names

Three states, named after who is waiting on whom. A card identity that is one plain word — and a two-layer claim scheme that makes two promises it can actually keep.

1Look and chrome

A dark-themed TUI on the Tokyo Night palette, designed against a monospace reference font (JetBrains Mono — though the program cannot force which font the user's terminal renders with) R6.

Open — the one live prototype axis

Whether cards and projects use borders at all, and where, is not decided. Three options are under test in a real terminal render:

  • (A) border on both the project box and each session card
  • (B) no borders anywhere — grouping by colour stripe plus a header line only
  • (C) border on the project box only; cards inside are plain

Whichever wins must avoid empty container waste and keep grouping unambiguous at narrow widths. Note the tension with R5.11, which reports that the current layout has no borders at all — and with R6's own “nested, padded, rounded-corner boxes, not flat tiles”. This is open item 1.

2What a card shows

Three lines R6.3:

  1. Title / handle line — the session's nickname plus its wire title (the harness's own session title). Both are always present; whether they sit in a border title or share a plain text line depends on which chrome option wins.
  2. Status + elapsed — e.g. needs-you · question, needs-you · 14m, running · 2m ago.
  3. Current action — one truncated line, e.g. editing: foo.rs or npm test. It holds its last value until new activity replaces it.

Cost and token counts were considered for a fourth line and cut by direct decision — which also resolved a contradiction with the cost-analytics non-goal.

Open — a real tension between two specs

visuals.md R6.3 says a card shows exactly 3 lines. layout.md R5.3 says tile content is a continuous function of the tile's size, from colour-only up through compact up through an extended multi-block form. These cannot both be true at large tile sizes. Not reconciled — flagged for whoever settles which rule governs card content as tiles grow.

3The attention model

R6.7 — confirmed final

Three states, not four. Every session is in exactly one. They are named for who is waiting on whom.

The three states.
StateMeansShowsOrdering
running A turn is in progress. The live current-action line. Not urgent — sorts after needs-you.
needs-you The turn ended; the harness is waiting on you. See the two sub-states below. Sorts before running.
idle Outside the active window W — but the session still exists. Never hidden silently if its project has ≥1 active session; otherwise an overflow chip. Context only.

The two needs-you sub-states

Same underlying status, two presentations R6.7:

  • question badge — the ended turn's final assistant text looks like a question. Distinct badge and colour, and sorted first among all sessions. This is the case the user called out directly: an agent waiting on an answer should be prominently highlighted.
  • needs-you Nm — ended turn, heuristic didn't match. Elapsed since the turn ended is shown directly, and that number is the urgency ordering — longest-waiting sorts earlier. No separate tier is needed.
Why the question state is a guess at all

Confirmed live: a session told to stop and ask a clarifying question ends with plain outcome: "succeeded" and time.idle set — wire-identical to a normal completed turn. Opencode has no permission or ask-question protocol signal. So the heuristic on the final text is the only way to tell them apart. It is evaluated once, on the transition into needs-you — not on every poll. (And per the adapter boundary, it lives inside the opencode adapter: a harness with a real “waiting on you” signal reports it directly instead.)

Why “stalled” was dropped, and why that was a real reduction

A fourth state, stalled, was considered and removed: no wire signal exists to detect it. This was not waved through — the user was asked point-blank whether they had ever had a session actually hang, as opposed to finish and sit waiting. The answer was no, and the reduction was accepted on that basis. Removing the state also removed the one thing the status-weighted-area toggle existed for, which is why that toggle is now unnecessary R6.1.

Why the names changed

An earlier draft used waiting as a machine state, which was ambiguous — the agent waiting, or you waiting? The three names answer that question in the label itself.

4The naming scheme

Every card's identity is a single word drawn from a categorized, curated wordlist. Words are never combined — no adjective+noun pairing. One plain word per card.

Names are handed out by a two-layer claim scheme. Both layers work the same way: prefer a hash-derived slot, and if that slot is taken, move deterministically — never randomly — to another one.

The cooldown, in detail

When a session ends, its word does not go back into the pool. It enters cooldown: it isn't claimable again until enough other distinct words in that category have been claimed since it was freed.

The mechanism is deliberately cheap — a small per-word “last freed” counter compared against a per-category “claims made since” counter. Bounded state, no historical log.

Why cooldown exists

Without it, a name could start pointing at a different session moments after the original one ends. The user's mental pointer — “go look at Apollo” — would silently retarget to something else. Cooldown buys enough distance that a name doesn't change meaning while it's still in working memory.

A worked example

  1. Project opencode-mcp becomes live. It hashes to category index 4 — chess. Another live project already holds chess, so layer 1 probes forward and claims index 5, Mollywood.
  2. A session starts inside it. Its identity (opencode, ses_9f2…) hashes to word 3 in the Mollywood list → Dasan. Dasan is free and off cooldown, so it is claimed.
  3. A second session starts. It also hashes to Dasan. Dasan is held by a live sibling, so layer 2 probes forward by its deterministic stride and lands on the next word that is both free and off cooldown.
  4. The first session ends. Dasan enters cooldown — it is now free but not claimable.
  5. A third session arrives whose hash lands on Dasan. It is skipped — not because it is taken, but because it is cooling — and the probe moves it on. Dasan only becomes claimable once enough other Mollywood words have been cycled through.

5What it guarantees

Both hard
  • No two sessions in the same live project ever show the same name.
  • No two live projects ever show the same name.

The second guarantee is the interesting one, and it is the entire reason layer 1 exists. Layer 2 only ever promises uniqueness among siblings within one project. Giving each project its own category — and relying on the curation rule that no word appears in two category files — turns a local guarantee into a global one.

Open — capacity edge case

What happens if a capacity assumption breaks — more live projects than curated categories, or more live sessions in one project than that category has words — is not specified. A numeric-suffix fallback (appending a number to a reused word) is noted as acceptable, since this is a genuine capacity condition rather than the common case the scheme is built around, but the exact behaviour isn't decided.

Coupling — affects the naming pool, not just display

Both claim layers must release their claim under R1.7's eventual staleness rule, not only on an explicit “gone” tombstone. Otherwise a session or project that goes silently stale without a tombstone keeps its claim and its seat forever, leaking the pool permanently. R1.7's threshold isn't decided yet; this coupling is recorded so it is visible when R1.7 gets designed, rather than discovered later.

6Why the 2026-09-01 scheme was reversed

The earlier rule was a deterministic adjective+noun nickname (both words ≤ 6 characters), hashed from session identity alone, which explicitly accepted collisions and forbade fixing them at render time (no appending hash characters). This new scheme is a reversal, not a tweak, for two reasons.

6.1 — Birthday-paradox math

The old rule was right for its own scheme. Adjective+noun is combinatorially large, so collisions were genuinely rare and cheap to accept. A single word drawn from one category's list of 10–14 words is a much smaller space: at real session counts, same-project collisions become common, not rare.

6.2 — Uniqueness cannot be a pure function of a session's own id

This is the deeper one. If two sessions prefer the same word, whichever one doesn't get it must display something not explained by its own id alone. So the name has to depend on arrival history — who else was live, and when.

The tradeoff, seen from both sides

The old rule advertised “pure function of session ID alone — no storage, no cache.” That property is provably incompatible with “collision-free and stable for the session's whole lifetime.” They are the same tradeoff viewed from two ends, and only one survives once uniqueness is required. The new scheme keeps uniqueness and deliberately drops purity.

6.3 — Two independent freeze levels

The wordlists are frozen once approved, and freezing now applies at two independent levels: the category list itself, and each category's own word list. Changing either after V1 reshuffles who holds which seat, because both claim layers depend on list order and length through the hash mod count arithmetic.

6.4 — Placement follows the chrome decision

Where the nickname appears is not an independent choice. If chrome settles on (A) or (C), a border exists and the nickname goes in that border's title. If it settles on (B), the nickname shares card line 1 as nickname · title. The wire title is shown as descriptive content either way.

7The word lists

Ten categories, frozen 2026-09-02. Each is destined to become its own file (wordlists/<category>.txt) once the dashboard crate is scaffolded; until then they are held together. Rules: ≤ 10 characters per word, no word repeated across categories. Ten categories rather than an earlier proposal of six, for headroom against the project-count capacity edge case.
